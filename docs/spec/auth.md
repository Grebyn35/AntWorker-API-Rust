# Spec: `auth`

## Purpose

`auth` proves who a caller is. It owns sign-in methods and sessions, and it implements the `SessionVerifier` that `platform::authn` uses on every protected request. It serves both kinds of client (D61): one that sets its own headers, such as the desktop client, holds its tokens and sends the Bearer header, and a browser holds them in cookies it can't read. This spec covers registration and sign-in with a password, refreshing, reading and ending the current session, authenticating requests, re-authenticating with the password, confirming the deletion of an account, which `accounts` then carries out, and the request of an export of its data, which `accounts` then builds, email verification, resetting and changing the password, a user's sessions, which they list and end, one or all but the current one, with the user events that tell their streams a session started or ended, and which an hourly job deletes once they stop lasting, changing the account's email address, with a code sent to the new one, and second factors: an authenticator app's codes, with recovery codes, which a sign-in, a re-authentication and a change of the password ask for beside the password, and a reset beside its code, which a deployment may require of every user, and which the operator removes from an account whose user lost theirs. The full module's other part arrives next: OAuth with identity linking. A provider's sign-in proves an address, as email verification and a reset do, so it comes after them, and after second factors, so that a provider's sign-in asks a user who has one for it from its start, as it asks at every other way in, as requirement 6.3.4 of the Application Security Verification Standard (ASVS) asks.

## Owns

| Table | Holds |
|---|---|
| `identities` | A user's sign-in methods. `id`, `user_id`, `method`, `created_at`, `updated_at` and `version`. For a password: `method = 'password'`, and `password_hash` in the string format of the Password Hashing Competition (PHC). `(user_id, method)` is unique. |
| `sessions` | `id`, `user_id`, `device_id` (optional), `device_name` (optional, personal data, AUTH-042), `access_token_hash`, `refresh_token_hash`, `previous_refresh_token_hash`, `created_at`, `updated_at`, `version`, `access_token_expires_at`, `refresh_token_expires_at`, `expires_at`, and `authenticated_at`, when the user last proved who they are in the session: their password, with their second factor when they have one (Re-authentication). Each hash is a SHA-256 of a token and has a unique index. `(user_id, device_id)` is unique where `device_id` is set. |
| `device_tokens` | `id`, `user_id`, `email_hash`, `token_hash`, `created_at`, `expires_at`. `token_hash` is a SHA-256 of the token, with a unique index; `email_hash` is the hash-based message authentication code (HMAC) under `HMAC_KEY` of the email the token was issued for (AUTH-019). Rows are never edited, and `app` may not update them (DATA-19). |
| `verification_codes` | `id`, `user_id`, `purpose`, `code_hash`, `created_at`, `expires_at`: the code that proves an account's email address (AUTH-030), that resets its password (AUTH-036), or that changes its address to another (AUTH-050). `purpose` is the Postgres enum `verification_purpose`, which holds `email_verification`, `password_reset` and `email_change` (DATA-04). `code_hash` is the HMAC under `HMAC_KEY` of the user's ID, the purpose, the address the code was sent to and the code's characters (SEC-03). An account holds one code that proves its address at a time, and one that changes it, which a unique index on `user_id` and `purpose` where the purpose isn't `password_reset` keeps, and a reset code for each request that lasts, since issuing one deletes those that expired (AUTH-036). Rows are never edited, and `app` may not update them (DATA-19). |
| `totp_factors` | A user's authenticator app, one at most (Second factors). `id`, `user_id`, which is unique, `key_version` and `secret`, the app's secret, encrypted under that version of `ENCRYPTION_KEYS` (SEC-02), `last_used_step`, the last 30-second step whose code it accepted (AUTH-057), `created_at`, `updated_at` and `version`. |
| `totp_setups` | A setup of an authenticator app that hasn't been confirmed, one at most per user: `id`, `user_id`, which is unique, `key_version`, `secret`, encrypted as a factor's is, `created_at` and `expires_at` (AUTH-055). Rows are never edited, and `app` may not update them (DATA-19). |
| `recovery_codes` | A user's recovery codes, ten at most: `id`, `user_id`, `code_hash`, the SHA-256 of the user's ID and the code's characters (AUTH-062), and `created_at`. Rows are never edited, and `app` may not update them (DATA-19). |
| `sign_in_challenges` | The challenge of a sign-in that waits for its second factor (AUTH-058): `id`, `user_id`, `token_hash`, a SHA-256 of its token, with a unique index, `device_id` and `device_name` (personal data), as the session it starts will hold them, `device_token_hash`, the SHA-256 of the device token that the sign-in brought, when it was valid for the email (AUTH-019), `created_at` and `expires_at`. Rows are never edited, and `app` may not update them (DATA-19). |

## Interface

- `SessionVerifier`, implemented for `platform::authn`: `verify`, which also says whether the session's account must still prove its email address (AUTH-025), and `lasting`, for the hub's re-check of its streams (AUTH-018).
  Its `session` serves a signed URL (AUTH-020). <!-- part: files -->
- The domain type `Password` (D25).
- `Contract`, the personal data contract for `identities`, `sessions`, `device_tokens`, `verification_codes`, `totp_factors`, `totp_setups`, `recovery_codes` and `sign_in_challenges` (D31), which exports them without their hashes and secrets ([accounts spec](accounts.md#what-an-export-holds)), and deletes them.
- `register`, which registers the job `auth.send_verification_code` (AUTH-030, AUTH-036, AUTH-050) and the periodic jobs `auth.expire_sessions` (AUTH-047), `auth.expire_unfinished` (AUTH-067) and `auth.reencrypt_secrets` (AUTH-066).
- `remove_second_factor`, which the binary's subcommand `remove-second-factor` runs (AUTH-072, D135).
- `Principal` is built here only, after a successful credential check or a registration: clippy bans its constructor, and `auth` alone allows it, through `#[expect]`.

## Design

### Registration

1. With email on, the registration counts in the bucket of its address (AUTH-029), since it sends the address a code. The password is hashed on a blocking thread, and the service opens the unit of work.
2. `accounts::create` inserts the user, or reports that the email is in use: the unit of work rolls back, and the answer is AUTH-002.
3. The password identity and the session are inserted, which publishes `session.created` (AUTH-046), the `Principal` of the new session is built, `user.registered` is recorded with its audit scope, and the device token is issued (AUTH-019).
4. The unit of work commits, and the route delivers the new session's tokens and the device token (AUTH-015).

### Sign-in

1. A device token that the sign-in brings, in its body or else in its email's cookie (AUTH-015), is looked up by its hash, its email's HMAC and its expiry, before any limit counts the attempt, since it decides which limit does, in a unit of work of its own that writes nothing (ARCH-11, AUTH-019). A valid one's rate-limit bucket is counted, keyed by its hash. Otherwise, the email's buckets are counted, each in its own short transaction (AUTH-006, PLATFORM-011): the one of the email from the client's address, and then, only when that one lets the attempt through, the one of the email from every address. A bucket's key is an HMAC of the normalized email (ACCOUNTS-002), so every spelling of an address shares its buckets, and in the first bucket's, of the client's address too, as the IP limit counts it (PLATFORM-054). The [threat model](../operations/threat-model.md#sign-in-limits) says how the two keep an attacker from locking an account's owner out.
2. The service opens the unit of work. `accounts::find_by_email` returns the user's ID, if any, and the password identity is read by that user's ID and locked until the unit of work ends, so the sign-ins of one user take turns (DATA-20): of two on one device, the second ends the session the first started (AUTH-008).
3. The password is verified on a blocking thread. When there is no user or no password identity, a dummy hash computed at startup with the same parameters is verified instead (AUTH-005).
4. On a wrong password for an existing user, `sign_in.failed` is recorded in that account's log through `AuditScope::attempt`, and the unit of work commits and answers 401 (ARCH-11). With no user, the unit of work rolls back, and the answer is the same 401.
5. On success, for an account with an authenticator app, the password is rehashed if needed, a challenge is issued and recorded, and the unit of work commits, with no session and no device token (AUTH-058), which the challenge's completion starts and issues (AUTH-059, Second factors). For any other account, a session with the same device ID ends (AUTH-008), the new session is created, `session.created` is recorded and published (AUTH-046), the password is rehashed if needed, which records `password.rehashed` (AUTH-014), the next device token is issued, which records `device_token.issued` (AUTH-019), and the unit of work commits.

### Refresh

The presented token is hashed with SHA-256, and the session is read by `refresh_token_hash`. If no session matches, it is read by `previous_refresh_token_hash`, and a match there is reuse (AUTH-012). Rotation updates the session's hashes with `WHERE refresh_token_hash = $presented`, so two concurrent refreshes can't both succeed. A refresh that reads its token from the cookie is a request the cookie authenticates, so the cross-site protection of PLATFORM-059 applies to it.

### Verifying access tokens

Every authenticated request reads its session by its access token's hash, with no cache, so a session that ends, by signing out or by a refresh token's reuse, refuses its token at once (D5, AUTH-013). A cache in each replica would need the access-change signals to drop what it holds, which only the hub receives. `just load-test me` measures what verifying costs (OPS-16).

`SessionVerifier::lasting` reads the sessions it is given by their IDs, which came from the hub's streams' principals, and keeps those whose refresh token and absolute lifetime haven't expired: a session lasts while it can still be refreshed, so a stream outlives its access token, but not its session (AUTH-018, SEC-06).

### Token delivery

One function delivers a session's tokens for registration, sign-in and refresh (D18), in the body or as cookies. A request that carries `Origin` comes from a browser, since only a browser sends it (PLATFORM-059), and gets cookies, since a token in the body would be readable by any script on its page, while a cookie that is `HttpOnly` isn't. Any other client gets the tokens in the body. A refresh decides the same way, wherever its old token came from, so a browser never reads a token, even one it sends in the body. `__Host-` makes a browser refuse the cookie unless it is `Secure`, has `Path=/` and names no `Domain`, so only the API's own host can set it, and the browser sends it to that host alone. The refresh cookie therefore goes with every request to the API too, as the access cookie does, rather than only to the refresh route: requirement 3.3.3 of the Application Security Verification Standard (ASVS) asks for `__Host-` on every cookie that no other host shares, and `Path` can't narrow such a cookie. Only the refresh route reads it, and neither the API nor its proxy logs a cookie. Signing out sets both cookies again, empty and expired, which removes them from the browser.

A browser sends a cookie that is `SameSite=Strict` only with requests from the cookie's own site, so a web client is served from the same site as the API, such as `app.example.com` beside `api.example.com`, and its origin is listed in `CORS_ALLOWED_ORIGINS` (PLATFORM-058).

### Passwords

The breached-password list holds at least the 3,000 most common passwords of 8 to 128 characters, drawn from passwords that breaches revealed, as requirements 6.2.4 and 6.2.12 of ASVS ask. The context list holds the words a password of this product mustn't contain, such as the product's name, which `just rename` rewrites (ARCH-15); a fork adds its own, as requirements 6.1.2 and 6.2.11 ask. Both lists are compiled into the binary, so checking a password reaches no other system. No rule asks for a kind of character (6.2.5), and the password is verified exactly as the user typed it (6.2.8).

### Re-authentication

A sensitive action, such as deleting the account or ending another of the user's sessions, needs its caller to have proved who they are just before, not only to hold a session, whose tokens a thief may hold: their password, and their second factor when they have one, the full re-authentication that requirement 7.5.1 of ASVS asks for. Each session records in `authenticated_at` when its user last proved who they are in it: when a registration or a sign-in started it, a re-authentication, or a change of the password through it (AUTH-040). A refresh leaves it. The action checks, in SQL against the database's clock, that it lies within the last 5 minutes (DATA-03), a fixed window, so a client asks for the password just before the action and sends the action at once. A session that is gone before the check reads it, because another request ended it (A user's sessions) or the hourly job deleted it once it stopped lasting (AUTH-047), proves nothing, and the action answers 401 `request.unauthenticated`, as a request sent after the end does (AUTH-009). A route of its own, rather than the password in each action's request, lets a `DELETE` carry no body, takes a second factor's code beside the password (AUTH-065), and lets a provider's sign-in, once OAuth arrives, re-authenticate a session the same way.

A check of a signed-in user's password counts in their bucket, opens the operation's unit of work, takes the user's turn in it, locking their row `FOR NO KEY UPDATE` through `accounts::address_for_update`, as a reset and a deletion of the account lock it first (A user's sessions), then reads their password identity, without a lock of its own, and verifies the password through `Workers`, or verifies the dummy hash when the account has none, so a wrong password and a missing one cost the same (AUTH-005, AUTH-021), then hands the operation its unit of work, open, so the operation writes what follows from the check in the transaction that read it (ARCH-11). For an account with an authenticator app, a password that matches is followed by the app's code, or a recovery code, which the check counts in the bucket of the user's checks of a second factor (AUTH-061), then checks against the app's row, which it locks `FOR UPDATE` to record the step it accepts (AUTH-057, AUTH-065). Every such check, re-authentication's and those the full module adds, such as changing the password, goes through one function and counts in one bucket of the user's (AUTH-022, SEC-13), so a stolen session gets 5 guesses in 15 minutes, whatever it checks. The turn makes two changes of the password take turns, so the second reads the hash the first set: without it, both would read the hash before either set one, both would pass with the same current password and answer 204, and the later's new password would hold, though its current password had stopped being the user's (DATA-20). A user who is gone once the turn comes, since a deletion of the account committed meanwhile, answers 401 `request.unauthenticated`, as the session went with them. A wrong password is a security failure, which the user's log records, and its unit of work commits that entry before it answers (DATA-22, ARCH-11); it answers 403 rather than 401, since the session still authenticates its requests, and a client that signs out on a 401 would end it. An account without a password, such as one the setup subcommand creates, can't sign in until its owner sets one through a reset (AUTH-037), so it has no session to re-authenticate. Re-authenticating serves an account that must still prove its address too (AUTH-025), whose owner corrects an address mistyped at registration through a change of the address, which needs the proof (Changing the email address).

### Password reset

A reset proves that its requester reads the account's email, as email verification does, and sets the password on that proof. It serves an account without a password the same way, which is how the owner of an account that the setup subcommand creates sets their first password ([workspaces spec](workspaces.md#the-setup-subcommand), D39), and it proves the address, since its code reached it. It needs email on: with email off, both routes answer 503 to a body that converts, so no client waits for a code that never comes (D9).

**Asking.** The request counts in the bucket of the address (AUTH-035), then, in a unit of work, reads which account holds the address, through `accounts::find_by_email`, and enqueues the job under that account's scope, which `JobScope::attempt` gives an unauthenticated request for the account it names, as `AuditScope::attempt` gives an entry, or under no one's, when no account holds it. Either way the request reads, inserts a job and commits, so its time doesn't tell which (SEC-05). The job's run issues the code and sends it as a verification code's run does, from that run, so the code is kept nowhere but in the email (D128); a run for no one issues nothing.

**The code.** A reset code is 16 characters of Crockford's base32 alphabet, the digits and the letters but I, L, O and U: the 80 bits of 10 random bytes. Its email shows it in four groups of four, such as `7KQM-2XWD-9RTB-H4NP`, and a confirmation reads it as Crockford's decoding does: without regard to case, without its hyphens and spaces, and with I and L read as 1 and O as 0. It lasts 10 minutes, the most that requirement 6.5.5 of ASVS gives a code sent out of band, works once (6.5.1), and holds only for its account, its purpose and the address it was sent to (6.6.2), as a verification code does, so a reset code sent before a change of the address resets nothing after it (Changing the email address).

Its length is what keeps its owner from being locked out (requirement 6.1.1). Its 80 bits, more than the 64 that requirement 6.6.3 suggests, let a check count only per email from each client address (AUTH-039), a count that no attempt from elsewhere fills: guesses from every address an attacker holds don't find the code, since each that the limits let through costs an Argon2id hash (AUTH-038), so 10,000 a second, all at one account, for a year, have about one chance in 10^12 of finding one of the few codes it holds at once, 3 for its requests of a window (AUTH-035). A code of 6 digits, as a verification code is, would need a count of the email from every address, small enough to bound guessing, such as AUTH-033's 10 a day, which 10 requests a day from anyone who knows the address would fill, keeping its owner from resetting on every device. A link that holds a token of 256 bits, as SEC-03's tokens are, would need no limit either, but it would need the address of a client's page, which the API doesn't know and a desktop client hasn't (D129).

**Asking again.** Each request issues a code of its own, beside the account's others, so a reset that someone else asks for cancels no code of the owner's, and each code works for its 10 minutes or until a reset uses one, which deletes them all, or a change of the account's address deletes them (Changing the email address). The bucket of the address lets at most 3 through in 10 minutes, a window at most as long as a code lasts, so the account holds at most a few lasting codes, and while the bucket is full, a code that it let through in that window still works and sits in the owner's inbox: no one who fills the bucket keeps its owner from resetting. A window longer than a code's life would leave, at its end, a full bucket and no code that works.

**Confirming.** The confirmation counts in its bucket, then hashes the new password through `Workers` before it opens its unit of work, so it costs the same password hash whatever it finds (AUTH-038). One that carries a second factor's code then reads, in a short transaction that writes nothing, which account holds the address and whether the reset code is one of its codes that last, and only when it is counts the second factor's check in that user's bucket of completions of a sign-in (AUTH-061), since what it reads decides whether that bucket counts the request (ARCH-11): a reset code that matches nothing counts in no user's bucket, so whoever knows only the address fills none. In the unit of work, it reads which account holds the address, then locks the user's row `FOR NO KEY UPDATE` through `accounts::address_for_update`, as a deletion of the account locks it first, so the two take turns. It reads the account's reset codes, and compares each that lasts in constant time (SEC-03). For an account with an authenticator app, a code that matches is followed by the app's code, or a recovery code, which the check of Second factors checks, locking the password identity, then the app's row: a wrong one records `second_factor.check_failed` and commits, as a completion's does, and a missing one rolls back, so either leaves the reset codes as they were (D136). Then it claims the code that matched by deleting its row, which only one unit of work can do, as a verification code's check claims its own (Email verification), then deletes the account's other reset codes. The job of another request's code deletes the codes that expired, holding no turn of the user's, so a code that expired while the confirmation ran may be gone by then: the confirmation answers 401 `auth.invalid_reset_code` and rolls back whole, the second factor's check with it, since `app` may not lock a code's row to keep it (DATA-19). Then it sets the hash of the password identity, or inserts one, which a sign-in locks before it starts a session, so a sign-in with the old password that started first has committed its session before the reset ends every session, and one that starts after finds the new hash (DATA-20). It ends every session of the user, each with its access-change signal, proves the address through `accounts::verify_email`, records its entries, and enqueues the email of AUTH-041. A failure for an account that holds the address records `password_reset.failed` and commits, as a failed sign-in does (ARCH-11), and one for no account rolls back: both answer the same 401, after the same hash, which is what their times share, as a sign-in's do (SEC-05).

**What it leaves.** A reset starts no session, so a user who has a second factor gives it at their next sign-in, and a reset bypasses none, as requirement 6.4.3 of ASVS asks. An account with an authenticator app proves it at the reset too (AUTH-037, D136): without it, whoever reads the mailbox could set a password that keeps its owner out until a reset of their own, though the factor keeps them out of the account. It deletes the user's sign-in challenges, since each was issued for the password before (AUTH-069). It leaves the account's device tokens, which only choose which limit counts a sign-in (AUTH-019), and a verification code, which proves nothing once the address is proved (AUTH-032).

### Changing the password

A change needs the current password and the new one, as requirement 6.2.3 of ASVS asks, and, for an account with an authenticator app, its code (AUTH-065), rather than a recent re-authentication, so a stolen session can't change the password without guessing it, and each guess counts in the one bucket of the user's password checks (AUTH-022). It goes through the one check that Re-authentication describes, which opens its unit of work and takes the user's turn at ending their sessions (A user's sessions) before it reads the password, then hashes the new password in it, sets the hash of the password identity, sets the caller's session's `authenticated_at`, since the request proved the password, or answers 401 when that session is gone, and ends every other session of the user, each with its access-change signal, so a thief's sessions end and the caller's stays. An account without a password sets its first one through a reset (AUTH-037). The new password may equal the current one, which no requirement of ASVS refuses, and which gives a guesser nothing. The route is a `POST` to an action, as API-03 gives one, rather than a `PUT` to the password, since a client may repeat a `PUT` on its own, which, once the first had changed the password, would record a wrong one.

**The email that follows.** A reset and a change each tell the address (AUTH-041), so an owner whose password someone else changed, through a stolen session, learns it, and resets it, which ends the thief's sessions; requirement 6.3.7 of ASVS, at Level 3, asks it. The email holds no secret, so `Mail::send` enqueues it in the unit of work that changes the password (PLATFORM-021).

### A user's sessions

A user sees where they are signed in, and ends a session they don't recognize, such as one on a lost device, as requirement 7.5.2 of ASVS asks. A user holds any number of sessions at once, the count that requirement 7.1.2 asks the documentation to state: one for each device ID they sign in with (AUTH-008), and one for each sign-in without one, each until it ends or stops lasting (AUTH-007).

**What a session shows.** A device ID tells a client's installations apart, but means nothing to a person, so a client also names its device, such as `Ada's laptop`, from what its platform knows, and the list shows that name (AUTH-042). The name is text the client writes, so it is personal data, as a user's name is: the export holds it, a deletion erases it, and no event carries it (API-19). The list holds the sessions that last, as the hub's re-check keeps them (AUTH-018): a session whose refresh token or absolute lifetime has expired signs nothing in, though its row stays until the hourly job deletes it (Expired sessions). It reads them through the index on the user and the creation time, the latest first (DATA-17), and matches `search` against the device's name as every list matches it (PLATFORM-047). `current` marks the session that authenticated the request, so a client that holds no session ID, such as a browser's page that reloaded, tells its own apart.

**Ending sessions.** Signing out needs nothing more than the session, as requirement 7.4.4 asks, so ending the request's own session by its ID signs out, cookies and all. The route is a `user` route, as listing is, so an account that must still prove its address, which only the routes that verifying needs serve, signs out through `current` (AUTH-025). Ending another, or every other, needs the password proved within the last 5 minutes, as a deletion of the account does (Re-authentication, D130), since a thief who holds one session would otherwise end the owner's: requirement 7.5.2 asks for a factor proved again before a user ends their sessions. Without that proof, the route answers 403 whatever its ID names but the request's own session, so the check comes before the session is read, and an ID that isn't a UUID answers as one that names none of the caller's sessions does (PLATFORM-072). `others` is a literal segment, as `current` is, rather than an ID, since a session's ID is a UUID. Each route answers 204, as a delete does (API-04), so the client reads the list again rather than a count. Ending sessions deletes their rows, whether or not they still last, as a change of the password does, so a session that has stopped lasting can still be ended by its ID, and ending the others leaves the user one row.

An end deletes its rows, then signals and publishes each, in one function that every request that ends a session goes through but a deletion of the account, which erases the user's events with them, and records each as `session.ended`, but a reused refresh token's, which records `session.reuse_detected` (AUTH-012). An end that deletes nothing, because another request, or the hourly job (Expired sessions), deleted the row first, records and publishes nothing, so it writes nothing that names the user, and never meets the user's row gone, when a deletion of the account deleted it (ACCOUNTS-013): a sign-out then answers 204, since the session is gone either way, and a reused refresh token answers as an unknown one does.

**Taking turns.** A request that ends the user's sessions other than its own, one or every other, or a change of the password, which ends the others too, first locks the user's row `FOR NO KEY UPDATE` through `accounts::address_for_update`, a change in its check of the current password (Re-authentication), as a reset and a deletion of the account lock it first, and only then reads its own session's proof. Without the lock, two such requests from two devices, each ending the other's session, would each delete a row that the other doesn't, both would answer 204, and the user would be left no session, which neither asked for (DATA-20). With it, the second waits for the first, then finds its own session gone, and answers 401 `request.unauthenticated`, as it would have had it come after the end (AUTH-045); one that waited for a deletion of the account finds the user gone, and answers the same. Every operation that ends sessions takes its locks in one order: the user's row, then the password identity, then the authenticator app (Second factors), then sessions, as a reset and a deletion of the account do. A change of the password sets the identity's hash before it touches its own session's row, and an end of other sessions touches no identity. A sign-in locks the identity, then the session on its device, and reads the user's row only `FOR KEY SHARE`, through a foreign key, which the lock on the user's row lets through. So a request only ever waits for a lock that comes after every lock it holds, and no two of them deadlock (DATA-20). A sign-out, a sign-in that replaces its device's session and a reused refresh token take no turn, since each ends only the session it names: of two that end one row, the second waits for the first's lock on it, then deletes nothing, as above.

**Expired sessions.** A session that stopped lasting signs nothing in again, yet its row holds its device's name, which is personal data, so `auth.expire_sessions` deletes it within the hour (AUTH-047, D131), as the other modules' jobs delete their expired rows, such as `accounts.expire_exports`. A session's refresh expiry never passes its absolute lifetime (AUTH-007, AUTH-010), so a session stops lasting exactly when its refresh token expires, and the job selects by `refresh_token_expires_at` alone, through its index, a batch of at most 1,000 rows in each transaction, as the platform's sweeps delete theirs. No access token outlives its refresh token (AUTH-007), so a session that the job deletes has no access token that still works, and a request that its token authenticated just before the session stopped lasting answers as AUTH-016 says. It spans every user by nature, so it selects by expiry alone, which `check-tenancy` lets `auth/jobs.rs` alone do (DATA-16). Its batch locks its rows `FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED`, so it never waits for a row that a request holds, such as an end of a user's other sessions, which may hold several of the user's rows in another order, and the two never deadlock (DATA-20); a skipped row goes at the next run. Scheduled maintenance records nothing (DATA-22), and neither signal nor event is needed: the hub's re-check closed the session's streams once it stopped lasting, and no request ended it (AUTH-046). Until the job runs, an expired refresh token answers `auth.session_expired` (AUTH-011), and an expired access token `request.access_token_expired` (AUTH-009); after it, either is an unknown token, and the client signs in again, as it would have either way.

**What it publishes.** Each session that starts publishes `session.created`, and each that a request ends `session.ended`, to its user's streams (AUTH-046), so the list on the user's other devices stays current without polling, and a client that sees a session it doesn't know can ask its user whether it is theirs. The payload holds the session's ID alone, and the client reads the list for the rest (API-19). The device whose session ended learns it as its stream closes with `session_ended` (REALTIME-005), and its next request answers 401. A refresh publishes nothing, since a session that refreshes neither starts nor ends, and a refresh every hour on every device would only make each list read itself again.

### Deleting an account

`DELETE /v1/users/me` is served here, since this module confirms it. The service reads the account's email, in a short transaction of its own that writes nothing, since the email names the bucket that counts the deletion (AUTH-024, ARCH-11), counts it, then opens the unit of work, reads, without a lock, whether the caller's session proved its password within the window, and calls `Deletion::delete` of `accounts`, which erases the account across every module and then deletes it ([accounts spec](accounts.md#deleting-an-account)). The contract of `auth` deletes the user's identities first, which a sign-in and a sign-in's completion lock before they start a session, so no session starts meanwhile, then their authenticator app and its setup, their recovery codes and their sign-in challenges, in the order of every operation's locks (A user's sessions), then their sessions, each with its access-change signal, so every stream of theirs closes once the deletion commits, then their device tokens and their verification codes, all before the deletion's last step locks the user's row `FOR UPDATE`, since a sign-in locks its identity, and a refresh its session, before it reads the user's row through a foreign key, and the job of a code that replaces an older one holds the older one's row until it commits, which the deletion waits for as it erases the codes, rather than at its last step, which waits for no lock. The insert of a job's code reads the user's row `FOR KEY SHARE` through its foreign key until the job commits, which the deletion's last step meets, as it meets an export's job, and answers 409 (ACCOUNTS-016). A code's check locks the user's row before its code, as the deletion holds it first, so the two take turns (AUTH-031). A browser's response clears the session's cookies, as signing out does; its device token's cookie stays, and counts as none, since no row holds its token (AUTH-019).

### Requesting an export

`POST /v1/users/me/exports` is served here, since this module confirms it, as it confirms a deletion of the account: the archive holds everything the user keeps, so the request needs the session that authenticates it to have proved who its user is within the last 5 minutes, the window of Re-authentication (ACCOUNTS-020, D137). The service reads that proof in a short transaction that writes nothing, before the export's bucket counts the request, since the proof decides whether the bucket counts it (ARCH-11): the bucket holds one export a day, so one that counted a request without the proof would cost the user the day's export for a missing re-authentication (ACCOUNTS-021). Then `Exports::request` of `accounts` counts the request and inserts the export ([accounts spec](accounts.md#exporting)).

### Device IDs

A client generates its device ID once per installation, such as from random bytes in base64url, and sends it with every sign-in, so a sign-in on the same installation replaces its session (AUTH-008, AUTH-017).

### Device tokens

A device token tells a sign-in from a device that signed in to the account before, so that attempts from elsewhere can't lock its owner out there (AUTH-006), as OWASP's "device cookies" do. A device ID can't, since its client chooses it, and anyone can send it. `device_tokens` holds each token's SHA-256 hash, with its user, the HMAC under `HMAC_KEY` of its email, with its own kind, and its expiry from the database's clock (DATA-03), so a sign-in finds whether its token is valid for its email before it reads the account, and a token that an attacker gets by signing in to their own account opens no limit of another email's. A token outlives the sessions of its device, as long as `DEVICE_TOKEN_SECONDS`, so a device that signs in again when its session ends still holds a valid one. A stolen token gives the thief its own limit's attempts (AUTH-006), until the device's next sign-in ends it, and still needs the password. A row is never edited: a successful sign-in deletes the token it used and the account's expired ones, and inserts the next, so a device that brings its token back holds one at a time, while a client that drops its token gets a new one with each sign-in, each lasting until it expires. A browser keeps each account's token in a cookie of its own, named by a keyed hash of the email under a kind of its own, so that signing in to one account leaves another's token in place, and the name shows neither the email nor the hash that `device_tokens` stores. The cookie authenticates no request, so the sign-in reads it whatever its `Origin`: a session cookie's refusal without a listed one (PLATFORM-059) guards what a cookie lets a request do, and a device token only chooses which limit counts the attempt, and comes as readily in the body.

### Second factors

Every user may add a second factor, and every superadmin must (D63); a deployment may require one of every user (D133). A second factor is an authenticator app, whose time-based one-time passwords (TOTP) RFC 6238 defines, with recovery codes for a lost phone (D134). For an account with an app, a sign-in, a re-authentication and a change of the password each ask for its code beside the password, as requirements 6.3.4 and 7.5.1 of ASVS ask, and so will each way in that the full module adds.

**The app.** A setup gives the user a secret of 160 random bits, the length that RFC 4226 recommends, drawn as requirement 6.5.3 asks, in base32 and in an `otpauth://totp/` URI, which a client shows as a quick-response (QR) code. The app's codes are 6 digits of HMAC-SHA-1, one for each step of 30 seconds, the parameters that every common app reads. A check takes the code of the current step, by the database's clock (DATA-03), as requirement 6.5.8 asks, or of the step before, so a code typed in the last seconds of its step still works, as RFC 6238 allows for the delay of its transmission. Requirement 6.5.5's 30 seconds is the step. Each step's code works once (6.5.1): the app records the last step it accepted, and refuses a code of that step or of an earlier one, so a code that someone saw over a shoulder works for no one else. The comparison is constant-time, through the TOTP construction of `totp-rs` (SEC-16).

**Setting it up.** A setup changes how the account signs in, so it needs the proof of Re-authentication, as requirement 7.5.1 asks. It lasts 15 minutes, which installing an app may take, and replaces the setup before it. Its confirmation, with a code from the new secret, proves that the app holds it. The app then takes the place of any before it, as a move to a new phone needs, and ten recovery codes replace any before them. A confirmation needs no proof of its own: only the caller who started the setup holds its secret.

**Recovery codes.** Each confirmation issues ten codes, shown once, each of which works once in place of an app's code (6.5.1). A code is 24 characters of Crockford's base32 alphabet, 120 random bits, in six groups of four. Requirement 6.5.2 asks that a lookup secret of fewer than 112 bits be stored as a password is, with a salt, and lets a standard hash store one of more. 120 bits let the database store each code's SHA-256 hash, as SEC-03 stores a token, so a check costs no Argon2id hash for each of the ten, and a code, which lasts as long as its app, outlives any change of `HMAC_KEY`, which ends the codes that last minutes. A user who has used some asks for a new set, which replaces the old, with the proof of Re-authentication.

**A lost factor.** A user who has lost both the app and their recovery codes can't sign in on their own. The operator removes their app with the subcommand `remove-second-factor`, on the deployment's server, with the image and the settings of the color that serves, as `just remove-second-factor` runs it over Secure Shell (SSH) ([deploy](../operations/deploy.md#removing-a-lost-second-factor)), once the operator has checked, their own way, that the person owns the account (D135). Requirement 6.4.4 asks for proof as strong as the setup's: the setup needed the password and a session, and the operator's check stands in for both. No route removes a factor on the proof of the address, since ASVS doesn't count an email as a factor (6.3.6), and whoever knows the password and reads the mailbox would get past it. The removal ends every session of the user, since a lost phone may hold one, records `second_factor.removed` with no actor, and tells the address (AUTH-072). Its unit of work acts for no request and no job, so its entries record no client, and it takes the user's turn, as the user's own changes do, so a removal and a request of theirs never deadlock (DATA-20).

**The secret at rest.** A check computes the app's codes from its secret, so the secret can't be stored as a hash. It is encrypted as SEC-02 encrypts a secret, under `ENCRYPTION_KEYS`, which the database never holds, so a copy of the database or of a backup gives no one an app's codes. The version that `ENCRYPTION_KEY_VERSION` names encrypts, the highest by default (PLATFORM-086), so a new key joins the ring one deploy before it encrypts, and no replica that a rolling deploy still runs meets a secret under a key it lacks. A check that accepts a code of a secret stored under another version stores it again under the current one, beside the step it accepted, and the hourly job `auth.reencrypt_secrets` encrypts the rest, so no secret needs a version once a run has passed since the deploy that made the next one current, while a restore can reach a moment before it until the backups' retention passes ([deploy](../operations/deploy.md#the-encryption-keys)). `serve` refuses to start while an app's secret, or a lasting setup's, needs a version missing from the ring, so a deploy that drops a version too early fails its health check before it serves, and the deployment keeps its replicas (AUTH-066).

**Signing in.** A sign-in whose password matches, for an account with an app, starts no session. It answers a challenge, a token of 256 random bits stored as its SHA-256 hash (SEC-03), which lasts 5 minutes, and which a browser takes in a cookie, as it takes its tokens (SEC-07). The client sends the challenge with a code, or a recovery code, to complete the sign-in, which starts the session and issues the device token, as a sign-in without an app does. The challenge holds what that needs: the device's ID and name, and the device token that the sign-in brought, when it was valid for the email. A wrong code leaves the challenge, so its user types the code again, and the completion that succeeds deletes it. A reset, a change of the password, a removal of the app and a deletion of the account delete the user's challenges, so no challenge issued with a password that changed since completes (AUTH-069).

**Guessing.** A check accepts two steps' codes, so a guess finds one with a chance of 2 in a million. Only a guesser who holds the password reaches the check, or one who reads the mailbox, since a reset checks the factor only once its reset code matched (AUTH-037), and each bucket gives them 10 guesses a day (AUTH-061): 3,650 guesses in a year, which find a code with a chance of about 1 in 140, through the bucket of the user's completions, which a reset's checks share, and as many through the bucket of their checks from a signed-in session, which needs a stolen session too. A completion with a valid device token counts in that token's bucket instead, as a sign-in does (AUTH-006), so a guesser who fills the user's bucket keeps the owner from completing a sign-in only on a device that holds no valid token, for at most a day.

**Taking turns.** A completion locks the password identity, as a sign-in does, then the app's row, which a check updates with the step it accepted, then its challenge and the session on its device, so it takes turns with sign-ins, a reset and a deletion of the account, which lock the identity before it. A check from a signed-in session takes the user's turn first (Re-authentication), then locks the password identity, before the app's row, since a change of the password sets the identity's hash after its check. A reset's check takes the user's turn too, then locks the password identity, before the app's row. A confirmation, a removal, and the operator's removal take the user's turn, then lock the app's row. Every operation locks the app's row after the user's row and the password identity, and before challenges and sessions, so none of them deadlock (DATA-20). `app` may not update a setup's or a challenge's row, so the hourly job can't skip the rows a request holds, as the sweep of sessions does: it waits for no lock, and leaves the rest of a run to the next once it meets one (AUTH-067). Of two completions of one challenge, both with codes that work, the second waits for the first's lock on the password identity, then finds the step used, or the recovery code gone, or its challenge deleted, and answers 401.

**What it leaves.** A setup, a removal and new recovery codes leave the user's other sessions, which they end whenever they like (AUTH-045), the option that requirement 7.4.3 asks for. Each tells the account's address, as a change of the password does, so that an owner learns of a change they didn't make (AUTH-068): an app that someone else added, from a stolen session and with the password, keeps its owner out once their sessions end, so the email tells them to reset their password and ask the operator to remove the app.

**Requiring one.** With `SECOND_FACTOR_REQUIRED` on, an account without an app is refused every request but those it needs to set one up, as an account that must still prove its address is (AUTH-025): `platform::authn` refuses it as it builds the `Principal`, so a route that a fork adds is covered without a check of its own (AUTH-070). The verifier reads whether the account has an app as it reads whether the account proved its address, in the read that authenticates each request, but only while the setting is on, so a deployment without it pays nothing for it. The routes that set an app up are added through `Routes::enrolling`, and those that verifying needs pass too, since they include reading the session, signing out and re-authenticating, which a setup needs. An account proves its address first. Every session's answer says whether its account must set an app up, so a client shows the setup first. While the setting is on, an app can't be removed, only replaced (AUTH-071). A fork then meets requirement 6.3.3 in full, through its configuration alone.

### Email verification

What an address proves follows whether the deployment sends email (D67).

- **Email on.** An account proves its address before the API serves it anything else: by entering a code sent to the address, or, once OAuth arrives, by signing in through a provider that vouches for the same address. Until then, every request of the account is refused with `request.email_unverified`, but those that verifying needs: asking for a code, entering it, reading its session, signing out, and re-authenticating and changing the address, so that an address mistyped at registration can be corrected (Changing the email address), and refreshing, which no access token authenticates (AUTH-025). The session that registration, sign-in, refresh and AUTH-016 return says whether the account must still verify, so a client shows the code's screen first, rather than deciding it from the capabilities endpoint (AUTH-026). Asking for a code answers the address it goes to, so a client shows where to look without reading the account's record, which is refused until then.
- **Email off.** No code can reach an address, so nothing asks for one, and no request is refused for it. An address is what its account claims, and the documentation of the email setting says so. An account that registered while email was off hasn't proved its address, so once a deployment turns email on, it proves its address as a new one does.
  An invitation reaches whichever account holds its address ([invitations spec](invitations.md#who-an-invitation-reaches)). <!-- part: invitations -->

**Where the check runs.** `platform::authn` refuses the caller, as it builds the `Principal`, so every route that a fork adds is covered without a check of its own, as is `Authorize<P>`, which opens with a `Principal`. The verifier reads the session by its access token, then the account's `email_verified` through `accounts::address`, in the same transaction, and tells `platform` whether the account must still verify, which it does only while email is on, so a deployment with email off reads nothing more. A route that verifying needs is added through `Routes::verifying`, which marks it, so a caller whose account must still verify passes there alone. The OpenAPI document lists `request.email_unverified` on every user and workspace route but those.
A signed URL's session is checked alike, since `SessionVerifier::session` says whether its account must still verify too (AUTH-020, PLATFORM-080). <!-- part: files -->

**Codes.** A code is 6 digits, the 20 bits that requirement 6.5.4 of ASVS asks of a code sent out of band, drawn by rejection sampling, so each of the million is as likely: a number of 32 random bits is drawn again while it is at or above the largest multiple of a million that 32 bits hold. It lasts 10 minutes, the most that requirement 6.5.5 gives such a code, works once (6.5.1), and holds only for its account, its purpose and the address it was sent to (6.6.2), since its HMAC covers them, so a code proves nothing once its account's address is another. ASVS doesn't count an email as a factor of authentication (6.6), and a code proves only that its account holds the address, but it is held to the same bar. A code's check is limited per user (AUTH-033), so a guesser gets 10 guesses a day at one code of a million, whatever codes they ask for: about one chance in 270 of guessing one in a year. A code's email can't be enqueued as `Mail::send` enqueues one, since the job's payload would hold the code, which SEC-03 keeps out of every row: the job `auth.send_verification_code` makes the code, commits its HMAC, and sends the email at once through `Mail::send_now`, so the code exists only in that run and the email. A run that fails to send retries as every job does (PLATFORM-021), and issues a new code, which replaces the one the failed attempt stored.

**Checking a code.** The check counts in the user's bucket (AUTH-033), then, in its unit of work, locks the user's row `FOR NO KEY UPDATE` through `accounts::address_for_update`, which says whether the account proved its address, as a deletion of the account locks the row first, so a check and a deletion take turns, and of two checks of one code, the second waits, then reads the address as the first left it, and answers 409 (AUTH-031, DATA-20). Then it reads the code. No row of `verification_codes` is locked, since `app` holds no `UPDATE` on it, which Postgres asks of `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE` (DATA-19). The code is compared in constant time, as `HmacKey::verify` compares (SEC-03). A wrong code is a security failure, which the caller's log records, and whose unit of work commits that entry before it answers (ARCH-11). A right one is claimed by deleting its row, which only one unit of work can do: the job of a newer code, which holds no lock on the user's row, may have deleted it while the check ran, and the claim, which deletes nothing then, answers that the code expired (AUTH-032).

**What it publishes.** A proof publishes no event: an account that must still verify opens no stream, since the stream's route refuses it, so no client of the account is listening, and the session that the confirmation answers tells the client that sent it.

### Changing the email address

A user changes the address their account signs in with and receives its email at by proving the new one with a code sent to it, as an account proves its first (D132). The address decides who reads the account's codes, and so who resets its password, and, with email on, what the API gives only an address's owner.
The invitations an account sees follow its address, so a change gives it those of the new one, and takes those of the address before ([invitations spec](invitations.md#who-an-invitation-reaches)), which its client reads again on `user.updated`. <!-- part: invitations -->

**Asking.** The address bears on how its account signs in and recovers, so asking to change it needs the password proved within the last 5 minutes, as requirement 7.5.1 of ASVS asks: the proof of Re-authentication, read in the request's unit of work without a lock, as a deletion of the account reads it (AUTH-023), rather than the current password in the request, so that a provider's sign-in or a second factor proves it the same way once they arrive (D123). The request counts in the bucket of the address asked for (AUTH-049), then reads the proof, and whether an account holds the address, which answers 409, as a registration does (D7), and enqueues for the caller the job that issues the code, whose payload holds the address asked for, which the job's run sends the code to. Nothing else changes: until a confirmation, the account keeps its address. An account that must still prove its address may ask too, and re-authenticate for it (AUTH-025), so that an address mistyped at registration, which its code never reaches, is corrected, and the change proves the new one.

**The code.** The code that proves the new address is 16 characters of Crockford's base32 alphabet, 80 random bits, as a reset code is, rather than 6 digits, as a verification code is. A verification code is checked by the one account that holds its address, so that account's 10 checks a day bound the guesses at the address (AUTH-033). A change's address isn't an account's yet, so any number of accounts, each made for it, may ask to change to it, each with a code and a bucket of its own: with 6 digits, each account an attacker makes would give them one chance in 100,000 a day at an address whose owner's place they want, and a count of the address across every account would let anyone who knows the address keep its owner from changing to it, as D129 says of a reset. 80 bits leave guessing hopeless whatever the number of accounts, so a check counts only in the user's own bucket. The code's HMAC covers the address it was sent to, so it proves that address alone, and a confirmation names the address with the code: the client knows it, since it asked for it and the request answers it, and the API keeps no address but its accounts'. The code lasts 10 minutes, works once, and holds only for its account, its purpose and its address, as the other codes do (6.5.1, 6.5.5, 6.6.2). Each request's code replaces the one before it, so an account holds one at a time.

**Confirming.** The confirmation counts in the user's bucket of code checks (AUTH-033), then, in its unit of work, locks the user's row `FOR NO KEY UPDATE` through `accounts::address_for_update`, as a code's check does, so that a confirmation, a deletion of the account and a reset take turns, reads the account's change code, compares it in constant time (SEC-03), and claims it by deleting its row, as a verification code's check claims its own (Email verification). It then deletes the account's other codes, each sent to the address before, and so of no use once it changes. A code that a job issues for the address before while the confirmation runs, whose insert comes after the delete read the rows, survives it, but its HMAC covers that address, so it resets nothing once the address changed (AUTH-038). Only then does `accounts::change_email` change the user's row. Its update of `email`, which has a unique index, holds the row `FOR UPDATE`, which waits for each operation that holds it `FOR KEY SHARE`, and which the insert of any row that names the user waits for in turn, as a code's job's insert does, which follows its delete of the code before it; with the codes deleted first, the confirmation waits for no code's row while it holds the user's row `FOR UPDATE`, and the two never deadlock (DATA-20). The unique index decides whether another account took the address since the request: its refusal rolls the unit of work back, the claim with it, and answers 409 (ERR-05). The change proves the new address, so an account that hadn't proved its address has proved one, and its session's `email_verification_required` becomes false. The change publishes `user.updated` and `member.updated`, as a rename does, since the address shows wherever the name does, records `email.changed`, and enqueues the email of AUTH-053.

**What it leaves.** A change leaves the account's sessions: ASVS doesn't count an address as a factor of authentication (6.6), so requirement 7.4.3, which asks that a user may end their other sessions once a factor changes, doesn't reach it, and a user ends them whenever they like (AUTH-045). It leaves the device tokens, whose email's HMAC is the address before, so they count as none for the new one (AUTH-019) until the devices' next sign-ins issue their next.

**The email that follows.** A change tells the address before it (AUTH-053), as a change of the password tells the address (AUTH-041), so an owner whose address someone who knows their password changed learns it, as requirement 6.3.7 of ASVS, at Level 3, asks. The email names the new address, since the owner signs in with it, or changes it back from a device still signed in, with a code that reaches the address before. It goes only to an address the account had proved: an address mistyped at registration may be someone else's, who would learn the account's new address. The new address gets no email beyond its code, since the confirmation that its owner sent answers that the change happened.

**With email off.** No code can reach an address, so both routes answer 503 to a body that converts, as a reset's do, and an address stays the one its account registered with (D9, D67).
